Consolidate, then Gate:
Continual Learning of Manipulation Skills Without Forgetting

A pretrained vision-language-action policy learns manipulation tasks one after another. Re-expressing each skill in a small core of its action expert, then switching the other units off for that skill's instruction and freezing the core, keeps every earlier skill exactly as it was — without storing any old data.

Summary

Setup

A simulated Franka Panda arm in ManiSkill 3 learns tasks in sequence from motion-planner demonstrations. The policy is SmolVLA (0.45B parameters): its vision-language backbone stays frozen and its 100M-parameter flow-matching action expert is fine-tuned. Each task is learned once and never revisited; after each task every earlier task is tested again on new scenes.

Policy inputOne 256×256 camera image, 9 joint positions (7 arm, 2 fingers), the instruction text.
→
SmolVLAFrozen vision-language backbone; trainable action expert (flow matching, 10 denoising steps).
→
Output50 future actions (hand displacement x, y, z and gripper) at 20 Hz; the first 5 are executed, then the policy looks again.

No privileged information reaches the policy: object and target positions exist in the simulator and in the demonstration files, but only the image, the joint positions and the instruction are passed on. Success is read from the simulator only to score test episodes, which use scenes never seen in training.

Method: consolidate, gate, freeze

The action expert's computation is split into 752 unit groups: in each of its 16 layers, 15 attention heads and 32 blocks of 64 MLP units. Every task goes through the same cycle; after it is learned and before the next task starts:

1. Consolidate. A new core is chosen at random among the unit groups that no earlier task has reserved (5% of all groups, 38 of 752). The task is trained a little longer with every other group silenced except the earlier, frozen cores, so the skill is re-expressed in its own core plus the earlier ones: 2000 updates for pushing, which recovers from {{cons_start}}% to {{cons_end}}% with only its core active, and 24000 for stacking.

Share per task. Larger cores keep more of each skill but leave fewer free units for later tasks. The results use 5% per task; other shares, from 1% to 25%, are compared with PackNet below.

2. Gate by instruction. From then on, task k's instruction runs cores 1…k and switches every other group off. The instruction is a normal policy input, so the gate uses no extra information.

3. Freeze. While later tasks are learned, every weight on these gated paths is frozen. A new task uses the whole network, including the frozen cores, but only the groups no task has reserved can change.

Because the earlier task's gated computation only touches frozen weights, its behaviour is preserved by construction — the frozen weights change by exactly zero. The experiments therefore ask what this costs: how well new tasks are learned, and how much capacity each task consumes.

The idea follows context-dependent gating with synaptic stabilisation in the brain and in networks (Masse, Grant & Freedman, 2018); in machine-learning terms it is close to PackNet and HAT, applied to whole heads and MLP blocks and keyed by the language instruction.

Inside one layer

The action expert is a stack of 16 standard Transformer decoder layers; the weight names are those of the code, which SmolVLA inherits from its language model. A query head reaches the residual stream only through its own 64 columns of o_proj, and each block of 64 MLP neurons only through its own 64 columns of down_proj, so switching off a unit's 64 values removes exactly its contribution. The gate does this with hooks on the inputs of these two matrices. Key/value heads are shared by three query heads each, so they cannot be switched off alone; they are frozen with the core heads that read them.

The same 5% under PackNet

PackNet also reserves a share of the network for each task and uses the task's identity to choose what runs, but it reserves single weights: the 5% largest of every matrix. Under the pushing instruction our policy runs a few complete units of each layer and switches the rest off; PackNet runs every unit with about 5% of its weights. Both freeze a similar share after task 1 ({{p_gate_1}}% and {{p_packnet_1}}% of the action expert's weights), but what runs for each task takes 752 bits to describe for the gate and an owner for each of 99.9M weights for PackNet.

Limitations

  • Capacity. Each task reserves a fixed share, so a fixed network holds a fixed number of tasks; a further task needs earlier tasks to give up part of theirs (next steps).
  • Larger allocations. PackNet is ahead at 10% and 25% per task: {{k10_packnet_avg}} vs {{k10_gate_avg}} and {{q_packnet_avg}}% vs {{q_gate_avg}}% after three tasks.
  • Scope. Simulation only, three tasks, one camera and {{n_seeds}} training seeds; each task is recognised by its exact instruction.

Reproduce

Everything runs from the repository on one GPU (an RTX 5090 trains two runs at once; the two-task study takes about 11 hours). Every reported run is one line in experiments/.

Hardware and software

Runs were trained and scored on two GPU types. Every machine used ManiSkill 3.0.1, SAPIEN 3.0.3, Transformers 5.5.4 and PyTorch 2.11.0. Repeated evaluations of a checkpoint on one GPU type agree (exactly, or within one episode in a hundred), but the same checkpoint scored pushing 91% on the RTX 5090 and 85% on an RTX 4090 (validation scenes; 8 of 100 episodes ended differently). Differences of a few points between runs on different GPU types, such as the gate against ER-500 after two tasks, against joint training after three, and the two methods at 10% per task, are within this sensitivity.
