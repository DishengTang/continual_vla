Consolidate, then Gate:
Continual Learning of Manipulation Skills Without Forgetting

A pretrained vision-language-action policy learns manipulation tasks one after another. Re-expressing each skill in a small core of its action expert, then switching the other units off for that skill's instruction and freezing the core, keeps every earlier skill exactly as it was — without storing any old data.

Summary
  • A SmolVLA policy, seeing only a camera image, its joint angles and an instruction, learns PushCube and then StackCube in simulation. Plain fine-tuning keeps 1.0% of pushing; experience replay of 50 stored examples keeps 85.7%.
  • Consolidate-and-gate, with 5% of the network per task, keeps pushing at 93.3% (it was 93.3% before stacking was learned) and learns stacking to 51.0%. Its two-task average, 72.2%, matches replay of 500 stored examples (72.5%, 94 MiB of old data); the gate stores none.
  • A third task asks for the opposite stack in the same scene. With each method at its best configuration (the gate and PackNet at 5% of the network per task), the gated policy averages 66.6% over the three tasks, the highest of the continual learners: PackNet 61.4%, replay of 500 examples 59.8%, of 50 examples 47.8%, fine-tuning 22.1%. It still stacks red on green when asked 43.3% of the time (fine-tuning 0.0%, building the wrong stack 63.0% of the time; replay 3.0–28.3%).
  • PackNet reserves a share of every weight matrix per task instead of whole units. At 5% per task, room for twenty tasks, the gate keeps pushing at 93.3% against 85.3% and leads by 5.1 points after three tasks (seeds 0 / 1 / 2: +1.7 / +6.7 / +7.0); at a quarter per task, room for four, PackNet leads (75.4% vs 72.3%). The gate's task records take 8.6 kB instead of a 99.9 MB per-weight map (details).
  • The price is plasticity: frozen units make new skills harder to learn (stacking 51.0% vs 54.7% for fine-tuning in the two-task study).

Setup

A simulated Franka Panda arm in ManiSkill 3 learns tasks in sequence from motion-planner demonstrations. The policy is SmolVLA (0.45B parameters): its vision-language backbone stays frozen and its 100M-parameter flow-matching action expert is fine-tuned. Each task is learned once and never revisited; after each task every earlier task is tested again on new scenes.

Policy inputOne 256×256 camera image, 9 joint positions (7 arm, 2 fingers), the instruction text.
→
SmolVLAFrozen vision-language backbone; trainable action expert (flow matching, 10 denoising steps).
→
Output50 future actions (hand displacement x, y, z and gripper) at 20 Hz; the first 5 are executed, then the policy looks again.

No privileged information reaches the policy: object and target positions exist in the simulator and in the demonstration files, but only the image, the joint positions and the instruction are passed on. Success is read from the simulator only to score test episodes, which use scenes never seen in training.

Method: consolidate, gate, freeze

The action expert's computation is split into 752 unit groups: in each of its 16 layers, 15 attention heads and 32 blocks of 64 MLP units. Every task goes through the same cycle; after it is learned and before the next task starts:

1. Consolidate. A new core is chosen at random among the unit groups that no earlier task has reserved (5% of all groups, 38 of 752). The task is trained a little longer with every other group silenced except the earlier, frozen cores, so the skill is re-expressed in its own core plus the earlier ones: 2000 updates for pushing, which recovers from 12.0% to 93.3% with only its core active, and 24000 for stacking.

A quarter of the network per task. Larger cores leave room for fewer tasks. At 25% per task the best configuration takes, in every layer, whole key/value bundles (a key/value head with the three query heads that share it, one bundle of five) and a quarter of the MLP blocks, so later tasks keep key/value heads of their own, and gives the third task a low-rank attention branch keyed by its instruction (rank 16, 1.3% of the action expert; without it 72.1%). It averages 72.3% over the three tasks (against PackNet).

2. Gate by instruction. From then on, task k's instruction runs cores 1…k and switches every other group off. The instruction is a normal policy input, so the gate uses no extra information.

3. Freeze. While later tasks are learned, every weight on these gated paths is frozen. A new task uses the whole network, including the frozen cores, but only the groups no task has reserved can change.

Because the earlier task's gated computation only touches frozen weights, its behaviour is preserved by construction — the frozen weights change by exactly zero. The experiments therefore ask what this costs: how well new tasks are learned, and how much capacity each task consumes.

The idea follows context-dependent gating with synaptic stabilisation in the brain and in networks (Masse, Grant & Freedman, 2018); in machine-learning terms it is close to PackNet and HAT, applied to whole heads and MLP blocks and keyed by the language instruction.

Rollouts after the last task

Against PackNet

PackNet keeps a fixed share of every weight matrix per task, chosen by weight magnitude after the task is learned; the gate keeps whole heads and MLP blocks. At 5% per task the gate is 5.1 points ahead after three tasks (66.6% vs 61.4%; seeds 0 / 1 / 2: +1.7 / +6.7 / +7.0), on pushing (93.3% vs 85.3%) and the newest task (63.0% vs 53.7%); red on green is level (43.3% vs 45.3%). At a quarter of the network per task, room for only four tasks, PackNet is 3.1 points ahead (75.4% vs 72.3%), on red on green (64.0% vs 54.7%). The two ways of reserving capacity differ in three respects.

Small budgets. The smaller each task's share, the further PackNet falls behind. At 5% the gate keeps pushing at 93.3% after consolidation; PackNet given the same 2000 retraining updates and the same state/action/time projections kept whole keeps 85.3%, and pruning those small projections too drops it to 44.3%.

Each skill is a separable network. A core is made of whole heads and MLP blocks, so a task's gated computation can be cut out as a narrower dense action expert. With attention-aware cores (a quarter per task; the random 5% cores mix query heads of different key/value heads, which the cut-out code does not handle yet), pushing uses 24.9% of the action expert's weights, red on green 48.1%. The cut-out networks reproduce the gated policy's actions (identical up to fp32 rounding) and, in closed loop under bf16, end most test episodes the same way; PackNet's per-weight masks leave every matrix at full size. The narrower networks are faster only where arithmetic dominates: on the GPU the 10 denoising steps are limited by kernel launches below batch 64.

Without freezing, stage B rewrites the core; without the gate, the units trained for stacking interfere. Either one alone keeps nothing.

Which units form the core matters little

A core chosen by intervention (the groups whose silencing hurts pushing most) can carry pushing on its own before any consolidation (seeds 0/1/2: 79/6/1% vs 24/0/0% for a random core of the same size), but after 500 consolidation updates both reach 93–99%, and through stage B a random core does as well: 72.3% vs 71.5% two-task average.

How much of the network each task reserves

Core size is the gate's capacity setting. Its memory stays the same (one bit per unit group and task), but a larger core keeps more of each old skill's computation and leaves fewer free units for the next task. Smaller cores leave room for more tasks, as long as the core can still carry its skill after consolidation.

When the tasks exceed the capacity

Both methods reserve a fixed share of the network per task, so a fixed network holds a fixed number of tasks. Here each method gives the first two tasks half of the network each, so the third task finds nothing free: it cannot be learned unless the earlier tasks give up part of their share, which they then lose. Neither method can add tasks beyond its capacity without that trade. Giving up half of each earlier share costs both methods red on green, but the gate gives up the unit groups that matter least and keeps pushing at 93.3% (from 96.0%), while PackNet's pushing falls to 41.0% (from 95.7%) (2 of 3 seeds).

The gate's other rows keep using the released groups, so task 3 changes part of the earlier computation; with distillation every update also shows task-3 images with an earlier instruction and keeps the predicted actions close to those before task 3.

The gated policy keeps the stacks it builds

Fine-tuning reaches a stack more often, yet the gated policy ends more episodes with the cubes stacked. Per-step traces show that every method often undoes a finished stack — in 51% of these cases by grasping the top cube again and lifting it off, since the demonstrations end at success and never show what to do afterwards — and that the gated policy undoes its stacks less often.

Control: how many predicted actions to execute

The policy predicts 50 actions and executes the first 5 before looking again. Five gives the highest or near-highest success for every method. Executing more of each chunk without looking lowers stacking and loses most finished stacks by the end of the episode; executing fewer stacks slightly less often but keeps more stacks. Pushing retention and the ranking of the methods hold at every setting.

Limitations

  • Plasticity cost. Frozen units make a new task harder to learn in the two-task study: stacking 51.0% vs 54.7% for fine-tuning. The reversed stack, learned third, reaches 63.0% vs 64.7%.
  • PackNet is ahead at a quarter of the network per task (75.4% vs 72.3% after three tasks), because the gated policy keeps red on green less well (54.7% vs 64.0%).
  • Capacity. Each task's core takes a fixed share of the unit groups, so a fixed network admits a fixed number of tasks; smaller cores admit more but keep less (three-task average 72.3% with 25% cores, 66.6% with 5%). Sequences longer than three tasks were not tested.
  • Selection on the test scenes. Configurations were chosen on the same test scenes they are reported on, by the same rule for every method; a separate validation split would give unbiased estimates.
  • Task identity comes from the instruction string. An instruction worded differently would not be routed to its core.
  • Consolidating a later task is not free. Re-expressing stacking in its core kept success at any step but lowered success at the final step (e.g. 21% → 12% on seed 0).
  • Scope. Simulation only, one fixed camera, demonstrations from a motion planner that sees object poses, 3 training seeds.

Reproduce

Everything runs from the repository on one GPU (an RTX 5090 trains two runs at once; the two-task study takes about 11 hours). Every reported run is one line in experiments/.

git clone https://github.com/DishengTang/continual_vla.git && cd continual_vla
export CVLA_ROOT=/path/to/large/disk
bash scripts/setup_env.sh && source scripts/env.sh
python scripts/download_assets.py && python scripts/replay_demos.py
MAX=2 bash experiments/run_jobs.sh experiments/01_main_study.jobs
python scripts/export_results.py --root $CVLA_RUNS/final --out results.json
